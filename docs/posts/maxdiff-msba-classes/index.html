Introduction

If you’ve ever tried to rate something on a scale of 1 to 10, you know how awkward it feels. Is this restaurant a 7 or an 8? Does everyone’s “8” mean the same thing? These are real problems — humans are notoriously bad at anchoring numeric scales consistently. They are, however, very good at one thing: picking a winner and a loser when forced to choose.

That intuition is the foundation of MaxDiff, also called Best-Worst Scaling. Instead of asking “how much do you like this course on a scale of 1–5?”, MaxDiff presents each respondent with a small subset of items (here, 4 courses at a time) and asks two questions: which is your most preferred, and which is your least preferred? The respondent repeats this across many different subsets, and the data from all those forced trade-offs is used to recover a latent preference scale for every item.

In this analysis, the 10 items are the 10 core MSBA courses at UCSD Rady. The 85 students who completed the survey each made 8 valid best–worst choices across different 4-course subsets. The goal is to estimate each course’s relative preference using three different methods — and see whether they agree:

  1. A counts analysis: the simplest possible summary — how often was each class picked best, minus how often it was picked worst?
  2. MNL via Maximum Likelihood (MLE): a principled probabilistic model that turns every choice observation into a likelihood contribution and finds the parameters that best explain the data.
  3. MNL via Bayesian estimation: the same model, but instead of a single point estimate, we use Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) to map out the full posterior distribution over parameters.

The three methods should broadly agree on which classes are most and least preferred. Where they differ — and why — turns out to be the most instructive part.

Counts Analysis

The counts analysis is the simplest possible summary of a MaxDiff survey. For each class \(j\), we compute:

\[ \text{score}_j \;=\; \frac{\text{times picked best}_j}{\text{times shown}_j} \;-\; \frac{\text{times picked worst}_j}{\text{times shown}_j} \;=\; \%\text{best}_j - \%\text{worst}_j \]

A score near +1 means a class is almost always chosen as the favourite when it appears on screen. Near −1 means it is almost always the least favourite. Near 0 means it sits squarely in the middle, or it is polarizing — loved by some, disliked by others.

The counts analysis is quick and intuitive, but it treats each “shown” occasion as independent and ignores everything we know about which other items were shown at the same time. That matters: being picked best when shown alongside unpopular classes is less impressive than being picked best in a tough lineup.

From MaxDiff Data to MNL Choices

The counts analysis is a useful starting point, but it has a blind spot: it ignores the competition. Being picked best when sharing a screen with three unpopular classes is less informative than being picked best in a strong lineup.

The Multinomial Logit (MNL) model solves this by modeling the choice process explicitly. We assign each class \(j\) a latent utility coefficient \(\beta_j\). On a task showing classes \(\{a, b, c, d\}\), the probability that class \(b\) is picked as best is the softmax:

\[ P(\text{best} = b) = \frac{\exp(\beta_b)}{\exp(\beta_a) + \exp(\beta_b) + \exp(\beta_c) + \exp(\beta_d)} \]

After the best pick, the respondent selects the worst from the remaining three. With utilities negated (lower utility → more likely to be worst):

\[ P(\text{worst} = c \mid \text{best} = b) = \frac{\exp(-\beta_c)}{\exp(-\beta_a) + \exp(-\beta_c) + \exp(-\beta_d)} \]

So each task contributes two log-likelihood terms — one for the best pick and one for the worst — and every item on screen contributes information, not just the one that was chosen.

Identifiability. Because softmax probabilities depend only on differences between utilities, adding any constant to every \(\beta\) leaves all probabilities unchanged. We therefore fix \(\beta_1 = 0\) (MGTA 403 as the reference) and estimate the remaining 9 parameters.

MNL via Maximum Likelihood

The full log-likelihood, summing over all \(N \times T = 680\) tasks, is:

\[ \ell(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \sum_{\text{tasks}} \Bigl[ \underbrace{\beta_{j^*_b} - \log \textstyle\sum_{j \in \text{shown}} \exp(\beta_j)}_{\text{best-pick term}} \;+\; \underbrace{-\beta_{j^*_w} - \log \textstyle\sum_{j \in \text{shown} \setminus \{j^*_b\}} \exp(-\beta_j)}_{\text{worst-pick term}} \Bigr] \]

where \(j^*_b\) is the class picked best and \(j^*_w\) is the class picked worst.

Code 
def log_lik(beta_free, *args):
    """
    Vectorized log-likelihood for MaxDiff MNL.
    beta_free: length-9 vector (items 2-10); item 1 is pinned to 0.
    Uses pre-built NumPy arrays — ~100x faster than a Python loop.
    """
    beta = np.concatenate([[0.0], beta_free])

    # Best-pick term: for each task, score of the chosen item minus log-sum of all 4
    utils_all  = beta[all_items - 1]              # (680, 4)
    best_utils = beta[best_arr  - 1]              # (680,)
    ll  = (best_utils - logsumexp(utils_all, axis=1)).sum()

    # Worst-pick term: negated scores of the 3 remaining items
    neg_utils_rem = -beta[remaining_arr - 1]      # (680, 3)
    worst_utils   = -beta[worst_arr - 1]          # (680,)
    ll += (worst_utils - logsumexp(neg_utils_rem, axis=1)).sum()

    return ll

MNL via Bayesian Estimation

Maximum likelihood gives us point estimates and standard errors, which is often enough. But sometimes we want to ask harder questions: what is the probability that Marketing Analytics has a higher share than Customer Analytics? or what is the 95% range of plausible shares for the Capstone? These are questions about the posterior distribution over parameters, not just about point estimates.

The Bayesian approach treats \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) as a random vector and updates a prior belief with the observed likelihood:

\[ \pi(\boldsymbol{\beta} \mid \boldsymbol{y}) \;\propto\; \underbrace{L(\boldsymbol{\beta})}_{\text{likelihood}} \cdot \underbrace{\pi(\boldsymbol{\beta})}_{\text{prior}} \]

We use a weakly informative prior: \(\boldsymbol{\beta} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\; 10 \cdot I)\). This says “we have no strong prior belief about which class students prefer, but we’d be surprised to see utility values outside the range \([-6, +6]\) or so.”

To sample from this posterior, we use the random-walk Metropolis-Hastings algorithm. At each step, we propose a small random jump from the current position, compute the posterior ratio, and accept or reject based on that ratio. Over many iterations, accepted draws trace out the posterior distribution.

Code 
def log_prior(beta_free):
    """Weakly informative N(0, 10*I) prior on the 9 free parameters."""
    return -0.5 * np.sum(beta_free**2 / 10.0)

def log_posterior(beta_free):
    return log_lik(beta_free) + log_prior(beta_free)

def metropolis_hastings(beta0, n_iter, step):
    K = len(beta0)
    draws  = np.zeros((n_iter, K))
    beta   = beta0.copy()
    lp     = log_posterior(beta)
    n_accept = 0

    for t in range(n_iter):
        beta_star = beta + np.random.normal(0, step, size=K)
        lp_star   = log_posterior(beta_star)

        if np.log(np.random.uniform()) < lp_star - lp:
            beta     = beta_star
            lp       = lp_star
            n_accept += 1

        draws[t] = beta

    return draws, n_accept / n_iter

Where the three methods agree. The ranking is rock-solid across all three approaches: Marketing Analytics (495) is first, Business Analytics (453) is second, and Customer Analytics (455) is third in every method. The same stability holds at the bottom: Analytics Consulting (444) and Mktg/Fin/Ops (451) occupy the last two positions regardless of method. When the signal is strong — a course that’s genuinely loved or genuinely disliked — all three methods converge cleanly.

Where they disagree. With this dataset, the three methods land on virtually identical rankings from top to bottom. That’s actually informative: it tells us the count of best and worst picks is already a very good signal for these data, and the MNL’s adjustments for lineup difficulty don’t shift enough items to change the order. In general — with noisier data, fewer respondents, or closer competition between middle-ranked items — you would expect more disagreement in the middle tiers.

What the MNL adds over counts. The MNL uses every item on every screen, not just the one that was chosen. A class picked best while competing against three disliked classes gets less credit than one picked best in a tough lineup. The counts analysis can’t distinguish these cases; the MNL can.

What Bayes adds over MLE. The MLE gives one number per parameter. Bayes gives a distribution. That distribution lets us answer questions like “how confident are we that Marketing Analytics’s true share exceeds 20%?” or “what’s the probability that Customer Analytics outranks Business Analytics?” — all of which flow naturally out of the MCMC draws.

Discussion

If an academic advisor asked “which MSBA courses do students value most?”, the answer from all three methods is consistent: Marketing Analytics (MGTA 495) is by far the most preferred, attracting roughly 28% of simulated best-picks if all 10 courses competed simultaneously — nearly three times the equal-share benchmark of 10%. Business Analytics (453) and Customer Analytics (455) round out the top tier. Students clearly gravitate toward courses with direct, career-ready analytics applications.

At the other end, Analytics Consulting (444) and Mktg/Fin/Ops (451) are consistently last — both attract far more “worst” picks than “best” picks whenever they appear on screen. The Capstone (454), interestingly, lands almost exactly in the middle with a near-zero net score: students are split, picking it best and worst in nearly equal measure, suggesting it is polarizing rather than uniformly disliked.

Which method would you use when? The counts analysis is an executive dashboard: fast, transparent, and easy to explain to anyone. The MLE is the right choice when you need a single defensible number to report — a \(\hat\beta\) with a standard error is something every statistician understands. The Bayesian approach earns its extra complexity when you need uncertainty on derived quantities (shares, ratios, “what if?” simulations) or when you plan to extend the model to individual-level preferences — a natural next step would be a hierarchical (random-effects) MNL that estimates a separate \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) for each student.

Caveats. The survey was completed by 85 MSBA students — a self-selected, program-specific sample. Preferences may shift depending on semester standing, prior work experience, or the specific instructor teaching a course that year. Item 11 appeared in a subset of tasks alongside a single class but with no worst pick, suggesting it may be an anchor or “none” option; excluding those tasks was the right call for a clean MNL fit.

If I had more time, I’d run the Latent-Class MNL — fitting two or three segments of students with different \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) vectors — to see whether there is a subgroup of students who rate the more technical courses (AI Math, Large Data) above the applied analytics courses. That heterogeneity is invisible in any of the three aggregate methods used here.

## Introduction

If you've ever tried to rate something on a scale of 1 to 10, you know how awkward it feels. Is this restaurant a 7 or an 8? Does everyone's "8" mean the same thing? These are real problems — humans are notoriously bad at anchoring numeric scales consistently. They are, however, very good at one thing: picking a winner and a loser when forced to choose.

That intuition is the foundation of **MaxDiff**, also called *Best-Worst Scaling*. Instead of asking "how much do you like this course on a scale of 1–5?", MaxDiff presents each respondent with a small subset of items (here, 4 courses at a time) and asks two questions: which is your **most preferred**, and which is your **least preferred**? The respondent repeats this across many different subsets, and the data from all those forced trade-offs is used to recover a latent preference scale for every item.

In this analysis, the 10 items are the 10 core MSBA courses at UCSD Rady. The 85 students who completed the survey each made 8 valid best–worst choices across different 4-course subsets. The goal is to estimate each course's relative preference using three different methods — and see whether they agree:

1. A **counts analysis**: the simplest possible summary — how often was each class picked best, minus how often it was picked worst?
2. **MNL via Maximum Likelihood (MLE)**: a principled probabilistic model that turns every choice observation into a likelihood contribution and finds the parameters that best explain the data.
3. **MNL via Bayesian estimation**: the same model, but instead of a single point estimate, we use Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) to map out the full posterior distribution over parameters.

The three methods should broadly agree on which classes are most and least preferred. Where they differ — and *why* — turns out to be the most instructive part.

## Counts Analysis

The counts analysis is the simplest possible summary of a MaxDiff survey. For each class $j$, we compute:

$$
\text{score}_j \;=\; \frac{\text{times picked best}_j}{\text{times shown}_j} \;-\; \frac{\text{times picked worst}_j}{\text{times shown}_j} \;=\; \%\text{best}_j - \%\text{worst}_j
$$

A score near +1 means a class is almost always chosen as the favourite when it appears on screen. Near −1 means it is almost always the least favourite. Near 0 means it sits squarely in the middle, or it is polarizing — loved by some, disliked by others.

The counts analysis is quick and intuitive, but it treats each "shown" occasion as independent and ignores everything we know about *which other items were shown at the same time*. That matters: being picked best when shown alongside unpopular classes is less impressive than being picked best in a tough lineup.

```{python}
#| echo: true
```

## From MaxDiff Data to MNL Choices

The counts analysis is a useful starting point, but it has a blind spot: it ignores the competition. Being picked best when sharing a screen with three unpopular classes is less informative than being picked best in a strong lineup.

The **Multinomial Logit (MNL)** model solves this by modeling the choice process explicitly. We assign each class $j$ a latent utility coefficient $\beta_j$. On a task showing classes $\{a, b, c, d\}$, the probability that class $b$ is picked as **best** is the softmax:

$$
P(\text{best} = b) = \frac{\exp(\beta_b)}{\exp(\beta_a) + \exp(\beta_b) + \exp(\beta_c) + \exp(\beta_d)}
$$

After the best pick, the respondent selects the **worst** from the remaining three. With utilities *negated* (lower utility → more likely to be worst):

$$
P(\text{worst} = c \mid \text{best} = b) = \frac{\exp(-\beta_c)}{\exp(-\beta_a) + \exp(-\beta_c) + \exp(-\beta_d)}
$$

So each task contributes *two* log-likelihood terms — one for the best pick and one for the worst — and every item on screen contributes information, not just the one that was chosen.

**Identifiability.** Because softmax probabilities depend only on *differences* between utilities, adding any constant to every $\beta$ leaves all probabilities unchanged. We therefore fix $\beta_1 = 0$ (MGTA 403 as the reference) and estimate the remaining 9 parameters.

```{python}
#| echo: true

# Reshape to task-level records
tasks = []
for (rid, tid), grp in df.groupby(["Record_ID", "Task"]):
    items = grp["Item"].values

worst_arr     = np.array([t["worst"] for t in tasks])          # (680,)

                           for t in tasks])
```

## MNL via Maximum Likelihood

The full log-likelihood, summing over all $N \times T = 680$ tasks, is:

$$
\ell(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \sum_{\text{tasks}} \Bigl[
  \underbrace{\beta_{j^*_b} - \log \textstyle\sum_{j \in \text{shown}} \exp(\beta_j)}_{\text{best-pick term}}
  \;+\;
  \underbrace{-\beta_{j^*_w} - \log \textstyle\sum_{j \in \text{shown} \setminus \{j^*_b\}} \exp(-\beta_j)}_{\text{worst-pick term}}
\Bigr]
$$

where $j^*_b$ is the class picked best and $j^*_w$ is the class picked worst.

```{python}
#| echo: true

def log_lik(beta_free, *args):
    """
    Vectorized log-likelihood for MaxDiff MNL.
    beta_free: length-9 vector (items 2-10); item 1 is pinned to 0.
    Uses pre-built NumPy arrays — ~100x faster than a Python loop.
    """
    beta = np.concatenate([[0.0], beta_free])

    # Best-pick term: for each task, score of the chosen item minus log-sum of all 4
    utils_all  = beta[all_items - 1]              # (680, 4)
    best_utils = beta[best_arr  - 1]              # (680,)
    ll  = (best_utils - logsumexp(utils_all, axis=1)).sum()

    # Worst-pick term: negated scores of the 3 remaining items
    neg_utils_rem = -beta[remaining_arr - 1]      # (680, 3)
    worst_utils   = -beta[worst_arr - 1]          # (680,)
    ll += (worst_utils - logsumexp(neg_utils_rem, axis=1)).sum()

    return ll
```

## MNL via Bayesian Estimation

Maximum likelihood gives us point estimates and standard errors, which is often enough. But sometimes we want to ask harder questions: *what is the probability that Marketing Analytics has a higher share than Customer Analytics?* or *what is the 95% range of plausible shares for the Capstone?* These are questions about the posterior distribution over parameters, not just about point estimates.

The Bayesian approach treats $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ as a random vector and updates a prior belief with the observed likelihood:

$$
\pi(\boldsymbol{\beta} \mid \boldsymbol{y}) \;\propto\; \underbrace{L(\boldsymbol{\beta})}_{\text{likelihood}} \cdot \underbrace{\pi(\boldsymbol{\beta})}_{\text{prior}}
$$

We use a weakly informative prior: $\boldsymbol{\beta} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\; 10 \cdot I)$. This says "we have no strong prior belief about which class students prefer, but we'd be surprised to see utility values outside the range $[-6, +6]$ or so."

To sample from this posterior, we use the **random-walk Metropolis-Hastings** algorithm. At each step, we propose a small random jump from the current position, compute the posterior ratio, and accept or reject based on that ratio. Over many iterations, accepted draws trace out the posterior distribution.

```{python}
#| echo: true

def log_prior(beta_free):
    """Weakly informative N(0, 10*I) prior on the 9 free parameters."""
    return -0.5 * np.sum(beta_free**2 / 10.0)

def log_posterior(beta_free):
    return log_lik(beta_free) + log_prior(beta_free)

def metropolis_hastings(beta0, n_iter, step):
    K = len(beta0)
    draws  = np.zeros((n_iter, K))
    beta   = beta0.copy()
    lp     = log_posterior(beta)
    n_accept = 0

    for t in range(n_iter):
        beta_star = beta + np.random.normal(0, step, size=K)
        lp_star   = log_posterior(beta_star)

        if np.log(np.random.uniform()) < lp_star - lp:
            beta     = beta_star
            lp       = lp_star
            n_accept += 1

        draws[t] = beta

    return draws, n_accept / n_iter
```

```{python}
#| echo: true

N_ITER  = 11_000
BURN_IN = 2_750   # discard first 25%
STEP    = 0.08    # tuned to give ~28% acceptance rate

    beta0  = result.x,      # start from MLE (faster warm-up)
    n_iter = N_ITER,

)
```


**Where the three methods agree.** The ranking is rock-solid across all three approaches: Marketing Analytics (495) is first, **Business Analytics (453)** is second, and **Customer Analytics (455)** is third in every method. The same stability holds at the bottom: Analytics Consulting (444) and Mktg/Fin/Ops (451) occupy the last two positions regardless of method. When the signal is strong — a course that's genuinely loved or genuinely disliked — all three methods converge cleanly.

**Where they disagree.** With this dataset, the three methods land on virtually identical rankings from top to bottom. That's actually informative: it tells us the count of best and worst picks is already a very good signal for these data, and the MNL's adjustments for lineup difficulty don't shift enough items to change the order. In general — with noisier data, fewer respondents, or closer competition between middle-ranked items — you would expect more disagreement in the middle tiers.

**What the MNL adds over counts.** The MNL uses every item on every screen, not just the one that was chosen. A class picked best while competing against three disliked classes gets less credit than one picked best in a tough lineup. The counts analysis can't distinguish these cases; the MNL can.

**What Bayes adds over MLE.** The MLE gives one number per parameter. Bayes gives a distribution. That distribution lets us answer questions like "how confident are we that Marketing Analytics's true share exceeds 20%?" or "what's the probability that Customer Analytics outranks Business Analytics?" — all of which flow naturally out of the MCMC draws.

## Discussion

If an academic advisor asked "which MSBA courses do students value most?", the answer from all three methods is consistent: **Marketing Analytics (MGTA 495)** is by far the most preferred, attracting roughly 28% of simulated best-picks if all 10 courses competed simultaneously — nearly **three times** the equal-share benchmark of 10%. **Business Analytics (453)** and **Customer Analytics (455)** round out the top tier. Students clearly gravitate toward courses with direct, career-ready analytics applications.

At the other end, **Analytics Consulting (444)** and **Mktg/Fin/Ops (451)** are consistently last — both attract far more "worst" picks than "best" picks whenever they appear on screen. The Capstone (454), interestingly, lands almost exactly in the middle with a near-zero net score: students are split, picking it best and worst in nearly equal measure, suggesting it is polarizing rather than uniformly disliked.

**Which method would you use when?** The counts analysis is an executive dashboard: fast, transparent, and easy to explain to anyone. The MLE is the right choice when you need a single defensible number to report — a $\hat\beta$ with a standard error is something every statistician understands. The Bayesian approach earns its extra complexity when you need uncertainty on derived quantities (shares, ratios, "what if?" simulations) or when you plan to extend the model to individual-level preferences — a natural next step would be a hierarchical (random-effects) MNL that estimates a separate $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ for each student.

**Caveats.** The survey was completed by 85 MSBA students — a self-selected, program-specific sample. Preferences may shift depending on semester standing, prior work experience, or the specific instructor teaching a course that year. Item 11 appeared in a subset of tasks alongside a single class but with no worst pick, suggesting it may be an anchor or "none" option; excluding those tasks was the right call for a clean MNL fit.

If I had more time, I'd run the **Latent-Class MNL** — fitting two or three segments of students with different $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ vectors — to see whether there is a subgroup of students who rate the more technical courses (AI Math, Large Data) above the applied analytics courses. That heterogeneity is invisible in any of the three aggregate methods used here.
